# Reviewing Notices & Letters

For documents issued *to* the user — not something they're choosing to accept, but a claim, allegation, or demand directed at them. The useful questions are different from an agreement review: not "what am I signing up for" but "what does this mean, is it done properly, and what should I do."

## Scope

- Warning letter (Surat Peringatan / SP)
- Show-cause letter
- Termination notice (Surat Pemutusan Hubungan Kerja / PHK)
- Demand letter
- Debt collection letter
- Notice of default
- Any other formal notice directed at a named recipient about a specific incident, obligation, or claim

## Review Philosophy

Assume the user wants to know:

- What exactly is this document claiming or asking of me?
- Was it issued properly — did the issuer follow the process they're supposed to follow?
- Is anything about it vague, unsubstantiated, or disproportionate?
- What are my realistic options?
- What happens if I do nothing, and by when do I need to act?

Don't rate the document's tone or fairness in the abstract — anchor everything to what the user should actually understand or do next.

## Step 1: Extract the facts

Before analyzing, pull out the concrete facts plainly stated in the document:

- Who issued it, and to whom
- Date issued, and any dates of the underlying incident(s)
- What specific violation, debt, or claim is being cited
- Any amount of money involved
- Any deadline for response or compliance
- Any stated consequence of non-response or non-compliance
- Whether it references any prior document (e.g., "following our SP1 dated...")

If any of these are missing from the document, that absence is itself a finding — legitimate notices usually specify most of them.

## Step 2: Procedural & Formal Check

Check whether the document follows the process it should, given its type. Flag gaps rather than asserting the document is invalid — you're not the authority on that.

**General checks for any notice:**
- Does it cite a specific, dated incident or obligation, or is the language generic/vague?
- Is it consistent with any prior notices it references (same violation, same amount)?
- Does it come from an authorized party (HR, legal, the actual creditor) rather than an unclear or third-party source?
- Is a deadline given, and is it reasonable relative to the stated consequence?

**Indonesian labor-law context — warning letters (SP) and termination notices:**
Common practice under Indonesian employment law (as generally reflected in UU Ketenagakerjaan and its amendments, and typically detailed further in a company's own *Peraturan Perusahaan* or *Perjanjian Kerja Bersama*) is:

- Warning letters usually progress SP1 → SP2 → SP3 before termination for repeated/ongoing minor misconduct, with each SP typically valid for a limited period (commonly around 6 months, but this varies by company policy) unless the employee re-offends within that window.
- Each SP is expected to reference a specific violation, ideally tied to the employee's contract, the company's *Peraturan Perusahaan*, or a *Perjanjian Kerja Bersama* provision the conduct breached.
- Serious misconduct (e.g., theft, fraud, gross insubordination) can sometimes justify skipping the SP1→2→3 progression and moving toward termination more directly — but this is fact-specific and process-sensitive.
- If a warning letter looks like it's building a paper trail toward termination, or skips steps, or doesn't cite a specific violation, that's worth raising — not something to conclude is unlawful on the strength of this review alone.

Present this as background the user can use to ask better questions, not as a ruling on whether their specific letter is valid. The concrete answer depends on their company's actual internal policy and the specific facts, which this skill can't verify. Point the user toward HR, their union/*serikat pekerja*, a labor law consultant, or the relevant *Dinas Ketenagakerjaan (Disnaker)* for a real determination — especially if termination is on the table.

**Indonesian debt-collection context — demand/collection letters:**
Indonesian financial regulators (OJK) maintain conduct standards for debt collection that commonly include: no threats, intimidation, or public shaming; contact limited to the debtor or their guarantor (not arbitrary third parties or the debtor's contacts/employer); restricted contact hours; and a requirement that the amount claimed be substantiated. If a collection letter's tone, contact method, or demands look like they cross these lines, note it as worth raising with the lender's compliance channel or OJK — again, as a prompt to ask the right people, not a verdict.

## Step 3: Red Flags

Look for:
- Vague or unsubstantiated claims (no specific date, incident, or amount)
- Missing reference to required prior steps (e.g., a termination notice with no SP history, when the situation reads like ongoing minor issues rather than serious misconduct)
- Disproportionate consequences relative to the stated violation
- Contradictions with earlier related documents
- Unusually short or unreasonable deadlines
- Threats or pressure tactics not tied to a stated legal/contractual basis
- Demands for payment or action with no clear breakdown of how the amount was calculated

## Step 4: Rights & Options

Lay out what the user can realistically do — request written clarification, respond formally in writing (and why doing so, even briefly, is usually better than silence), dispute the facts or amount, escalate internally (HR, a manager, the union), or escalate externally (Disnaker, OJK, a lawyer). Be concrete about *which* option fits *this* document rather than listing all of them generically.

## Deliverables

Produce the review in this structure (translate headers per the glossary in SKILL.md if writing in Bahasa Indonesia):

```text
## Executive Summary
What this document is, who it's from, and the bottom line in one or two sentences.

## What's Being Alleged / Requested
The concrete facts extracted in Step 1.

## Procedural & Formal Check
Whether the document follows the process expected for its type; gaps or inconsistencies found.

## Red Flags
Anything vague, unsubstantiated, disproportionate, or procedurally off.

## Your Rights & Options
Concrete, realistic next steps available to the user, matched to this specific document.

## Deadlines & Consequences of Inaction
Any stated deadline and what happens if the user doesn't respond.

## Recommended Next Steps
A short, practical action list — e.g., respond in writing by [date], keep copies of everything, raise [specific gap] with HR/the lender, consult [specific type of professional] if [specific condition].
```

Skip severity ratings and risk-count tables for this genre — they don't map cleanly onto "am I in trouble and what do I do," which is what the user actually needs answered.
